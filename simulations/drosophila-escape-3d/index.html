MaleCNS v1.0 · 167,106 neurons · biomechanical body

A fly that flinches, and the 185 neurons it needs

The whole released male-CNS connectome drives a biomechanical Drosophila body in MuJoCo. It sees through 1,466 retinal columns by casting rays into the scene — real optics, not coordinates handed to an encoder. A black ball approaches at 1 m/s, the giant fibre fires, and the fly leaps — about 9 mm straight up.

Then you remove neurons. Take out the 185 LPLC2 cells and the fly does not move. Take out 311 neurons at random — more cells, chosen blindly — and it survives untouched. That contrast is the whole experiment: without it, "break the brain and the fly breaks" is true by construction and means nothing.

The run

What is real here

  • The wiring. All 167,106 neurons of MaleCNS v1.0 and their 25.6 million connections, with the reference implementation's receptor model applied. Nothing is trained, nothing is fitted.
  • The optics. 1,466 retinal columns, seven rays each over the measured 4.5° acceptance angle, cast into the MuJoCo scene. The connectome sees geometry, not coordinates.
  • The body. NeuroMechFly: six legs, adhesion, every contact solved by the physics engine. The walking command comes out of the fly's own descending neurons.
  • The lesion. Removing a cell removes it from the computation, not from the picture. All four conditions start from a bit-identical state; only the lesion differs.

What is not

  • It does not fly. The connectome has no route from the octopaminergic drive to the wing motor neurons, so there is no flight state to enter. The wings are geometry.
  • The decision to jump is the connectome's; the jump itself is not. The giant fibre crossing 33 Hz is measured. What that becomes — 0.6 m/s at 45°, the reference implementation's own numbers — is a motor readout. In a real fly the giant fibre drives the tergotrochanteral motor neuron and the middle legs extend; that circuit is known but is not in the graph being integrated here.
  • Nothing catches the fly. There is no landing controller and no flight, so the leap is ballistic and the fly tumbles. That is what the physics does when a body is thrown and nothing flies it.
  • It does not forage, and it does not steer. DNa02, the steering neuron, sits under tonic inhibition in this model; the fly walks straight. That is a measured property, not an omission.
  • These are recorded runs. The simulation is 22× slower than real time on an H200 and cannot run in a browser. Every frame and every number was produced by the pipeline below and is replayed here unchanged.
  • One run per condition. The model is deterministic (synaptic depression off), so repeats of the same condition are identical. What varies across the random arm is which neurons it removes.

The protocol, and four wrong guesses

A 30 mm black sphere approaches at 1 m/s from 45° and stops 35 mm away — the reference implementation's own loom, transcribed. That stop matters, and finding out why cost four measured dead ends: the brain's timestep (10 / 2 / 1 ms gave the same answer), the radiance scale (32 → 35 Hz across a twentyfold range), spatial structure in the scene (a room built from 870 tiles made it worse), and the approach angle (23–46 Hz, with the peak always at the same distance). A ball that sweeps past the reference distance never lets the giant fibre build its response. With the stop it rises in 100 ms, peaks, and then adapts away — which is what a looming detector should do.

One measured aside, visible in the retina itself: 356 columns look out between 75° and 105°, against 150 within ±15° of straight ahead. A head-on loom lands in the sparsest part of the visual field.
